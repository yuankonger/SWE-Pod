## 1. Metrics: collaboration gain, overhead, communication efficiency, credit, robustness

### Takeaway
There is no single agreed "synergy" metric. Three different baselines are in use (best individual / nominal group, single agent with all information, equal-budget single agent) and they answer different questions; human team science has the oldest and cleanest definitions (Steiner's process loss, Larson's weak/strong synergy), while the LLM literature has only recently proposed a budget-normalised ratio (Γ, a 2026 preprint). Per-agent credit (Shapley-style) and fault-robustness metrics exist but are preprint-stage and not standardised; automated blame attribution is demonstrably unreliable.

### Cited Findings

**Collaboration gain / synergy**
- Equal-budget ratio. Fan et al. (Feb 2026) [PP, position paper] define collaboration gain Γ = Φ_M / Φ_S, where Φ_M is the multi-agent system's performance and Φ_S is "the best achievable results of a single agent when allocated a total resource budget equivalent to that of the MAS"; Γ is "the performance ratio of a MAS to a Single-Agent System (SAS) under equivalent computational resource constraints", with Γ = 1 the null of zero interaction effect and Γ > 1 indicating synergy. The budget unit is not fixed: "token throughput, sampling steps, or tool-invocation counts", "calibrated to the specific bottlenecks of each task". Their stated motivation: current task metrics "inherently conflate" "the intrinsic gain from collaboration" with "the performance improvement resulting merely from increased computational budget". — [arXiv 2602.05289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05289) (verified from HTML; appendix B.2 on resource equivalence not read)
- Team versus nominal group (human literature). Almaatouq et al. (PNAS 2021) [PR] define synergy as "performance in excess of what would be expected for a similarly sized collection of individuals working independently" (a "nominal group"), and compare interacting groups against the best, fastest and most efficient member of the nominal group. Finding: "Groups are as fast as the fastest individual and more efficient than the most efficient individual when the task is complex but not when the task is simple"; the balance of process losses and gains "switches signs at intermediate values of task complexity". Complexity is operationalised as number of task components and interdependencies. — [PNAS / PMC8433503](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8433503)
- Weak versus strong synergy. Larson (2010) [Book] defines synergy as "an objective gain in performance that is attributable to group interaction" and asks whether interaction "leads to better performance than would otherwise be achieved simply by combining the separate efforts of an equal number of people who work independently"; the book distinguishes weak and strong synergy and reviews evidence for each. (The publisher blurb confirms the two levels but not their thresholds; the usual reading is weak = group beats its average member, strong = group beats its best member. I could not verify that wording against the chapter text.) — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/In-Search-of-Synergy-in-Small-Group-Performance/Larson-Jr/p/book/9780805859447)
- Team versus best individual, empirical. Laughlin et al. (JPSP 2006) [PR], 760 students: groups of 3, 4 and 5 "proposed more complex equations and had fewer trials to solution than the best of an equivalent number of individuals"; groups of 2 did not; "3-person groups are necessary and sufficient to perform better than the best individuals on highly intellective problems". — [PubMed 16649860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16649860/)
- Team versus single agent with all information (equal-information ceiling). The hidden-profile design gives this metric directly. Human meta-analysis (65 studies, 101 independent effects, 3,189 groups): groups mentioned two standard deviations more common than unique information, and hidden-profile groups were far less likely to reach the correct solution than groups with full information; moderators were group size, total information load, proportion of unique information, task demonstrability and hidden-profile strength. — [Personality and Social Psychology Review, doi 10.1177/1088868311417243](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1088868311417243) [PR; abstract seen via search snippet only, page returned 403]
- Same metric for LLM agents. Li, Naito and Shirado (ICML 2026) [PR], HiddenBench, 65 tasks, 15 frontier LLMs: multi-agent systems reached 30.1% accuracy under distributed information versus 80.7% for single agents given full information; neither model scale nor individual reasoning accuracy reliably predicted group performance; a lightweight structured protocol (each agent surfaces new facts and challenges the leading guess) substantially narrowed the gap. — [PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html), [arXiv 2505.11556](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11556v2) (numbers from abstract/poster summary)

**Coordination cost / process loss**
- Steiner (1972) [Book]: actual productivity = potential productivity minus losses due to faulty process; potential productivity is estimated from member resources and task demands; task typology of additive (contributions summed), disjunctive (group as good as its best member if it recognises them), conjunctive (group as good as its weakest member). Only secondary sources were retrieved; a later formulation (Wilke and Van Knippenberg 1996) states it as an inequality. — [Wikipedia: Steiner's taxonomy of tasks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner%27s_Taxonomy_of_Tasks), [lecture summary](https://darkwing.uoregon.edu/~harrow/psy457w99/Steiner.html)
- Separating coordination loss from motivation loss. Ingham, Levinger, Graves and Peckham (JESP 1974) [PR] used "pseudo-groups" (confederates who only pretended to pull): performance dropped with perceived group size even when nothing had to be coordinated, i.e. part of the Ringelmann loss is motivational. A secondary summary gives roughly 90% of solo output in perceived pairs and 85% in perceived six-person groups (not checked against the paper). — [paper PDF](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/jaro2020/PSX_512/um/Ingham_et_al_1974_Ringelmann_effect.pdf?lang=en), [Sage encyclopedia entry](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/processes/chpt/ringelmann-effect)
- LLM-side overhead definitions are covered in the already-reviewed Kim et al. paper (arXiv 2512.08296); not repeated here.

**Communication / coordination quality scores**
- MultiAgentBench (Zhu et al., ACL 2025) [PR] reports a Task Score from milestone-based KPIs plus a Communication Score and a Planning Score, each rated on a five-point scale by an LLM judge; the Coordination Score is their average. It also compares star, chain, tree and graph topologies: "Graph structure performs the best among coordination protocols in the research scenario", tree performs worst, and "cognitive planning improves milestone achievement rates by 3%". The LLM-judge scores' validation against human judgment was not confirmed in what I read. — [arXiv 2503.01935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01935), [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.acl-long.421)

**Per-agent credit**
- Shapley-style credit in LLM agent systems (all [PP]; authors' own experiments): HiveMind uses Shapley values to score each agent and a "DAG-Shapley" approximation that prunes coalitions using the workflow graph, reporting over 80% fewer LLM calls for near-exact attribution — [arXiv 2512.06432](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.06432); SHARP (Feb 2026) uses a marginal credit term comparing the full trajectory's reward with the reward when one agent's contribution is removed — [arXiv 2602.08335](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08335); Shapley-Coop applies Shapley credit to self-interested agents, tested in ChatDev — [arXiv 2506.07388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07388); a conceptual proposal decomposes system reward into agent Shapley shares and then signed per-message rewards, with no empirical validation — [arXiv 2511.10687](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10687); an Aumann–Shapley variant argues attribution from small runs cannot simply be rescaled when the value function is nonlinear — [arXiv 2605.11404](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11404).
- Blame attribution is hard. Zhang et al. (ICML 2025) [PR], Who&When dataset (failure logs from 127 LLM multi-agent systems, annotated with responsible agent and decisive error step): the best method identified the responsible agent with 53.5% accuracy and the decisive step with 14.2%; some methods performed below random. — [PMLR v267](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/zhang25cq.html), [arXiv 2505.00212](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00212)
- Failure taxonomy as a diagnostic metric. Cemri et al. (NeurIPS 2025) [PR]: MAST, 14 failure modes in 3 categories (specification issues, inter-agent misalignment, task verification), built from 150 traces (inter-annotator κ = 0.88) and applied to 1,600+ traces across 7 frameworks (arXiv version says 1,642); LLM annotator 94% accuracy, κ = 0.77 against experts. The paper's premise is that MAS "performance gains across popular benchmarks often remain minimal compared to single-agent frameworks". — [arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657v2), [NeurIPS 2025 page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/121528)

**Robustness to a faulty or adversarial teammate**
- Huang et al. (arXiv Aug 2024; listed as an ICML 2025 poster) measure resilience as the performance drop when one agent is made faulty, using AutoTransform (rewrites an agent's profile so it makes stealthy errors) and AutoInject (inserts errors into messages), across six systems, three structures and four tasks. Hierarchical A→(B↔C) had "the lowest performance drop of 5.5%" versus 10.5% and 23.7% for the other two structures (v4; an earlier version reported 9.2% vs 26.0% and 31.2%, so numbers are version-dependent). An added Inspector agent recovers "up to 96.4%" of faulty-agent errors. Limitations noted: one shared backbone model, temperature zero, automated scoring. — [arXiv 2408.00989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00989v4), [ICML 2025 poster](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/poster/44721)
- In MARL the analogous metric is the self-play to cross-play gap (see section 3): SAD agents in Hanabi score 23.97 in self-play and 2.52 in cross-play. — [Hu et al., ICML 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/hu20a.html)

### Inferences
- The three baselines isolate different things and should be reported side by side: (a) equal-budget single agent isolates compute from collaboration; (b) full-information single agent gives the ceiling when the task is information-distributed and measures information-pooling loss; (c) best individual / nominal group tests "strong synergy". For a benchmark where each agent owns one service, (b) corresponds to one agent with access to all services and (a) to one agent with the team's total token budget.
- Steiner's decomposition implies a benchmark should try to estimate potential productivity (for example, per-service solo success on decoupled sub-tasks) so that process loss can be reported, and Ingham's design implies separating losses due to coordination from losses due to each agent doing less (for LLMs, the analogue of motivation loss would be reduced effort or premature deferral to teammates; this mapping is my inference, not an established finding).
- Shapley-style credit needs coalition evaluations v(S) for subsets S of agents; with four service owners that is 16 coalitions, which is cheap enough to compute exactly, but "removing" an owner requires defining a stand-in (no-op, scripted stub), and the result depends on that choice.
- Robustness should be reported as a drop relative to the clean team, with the fault type specified (role-level versus message-level), because Huang et al.'s numbers changed materially between paper versions.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was found that adopts Γ or any single synergy formula as a standard; Γ comes from a position preprint.
- I did not find a widely used, validated "communication efficiency" formula (e.g. success per message or per token of inter-agent traffic) in a peer-reviewed LLM benchmark; MultiAgentBench's communication score is an LLM-judge rating, not an efficiency ratio.
- Unverified leads from prior knowledge (not re-fetched; check before citing): the classical Shapley value φ_i = Σ_{S⊆N\{i}} [|S|!(n−|S|−1)!/n!]·[v(S∪{i}) − v(S)]; COMA's counterfactual advantage, which compares the joint Q-value to a baseline marginalising out one agent's action (Foerster et al., AAAI 2018, arXiv 1705.08926); Wolpert and Tumer's difference rewards D_i = G(z) − G(z with agent i removed or replaced by a default); Karau and Williams (1993) meta-analysis of social loafing (78 studies confirmed by a secondary summary at [typeset.io copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/social-loafing-a-meta-analytic-review-and-theoretical-22g4ic0jro.pdf); the often-quoted effect size d ≈ 0.44 came only from a blog); the "eight times less likely" figure usually quoted from the hidden-profile meta-analysis.

## 2. Controls: what goes wrong without equal-budget, equal-information or no-communication baselines

### Takeaway
Repeatedly, apparent multi-agent gains shrink or vanish when the single-agent baseline is given the same number of samples, a strong prompt, or the same model quality; and in the other direction, an equal-information control shows that LLM teams lose most of the value of distributed information. These are established for reasoning/QA tasks and debate-style systems; evidence for long-horizon engineering tasks is thinner.

### Cited Findings
- Equal number of responses. Huang et al., "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" (arXiv Oct 2023) [PP as retrieved]: on GSM8K, multi-agent debate with 3 responses scored 83.2% versus 82.5% for self-consistency with 3 responses; with 6 responses, debate (round 1) stayed at 83.2% while self-consistency reached 85.3%. Conclusion: debate's gain comes from aggregating multiple generations, not from agents correcting each other. — [arXiv 2310.01798](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798)
- Strong-prompt single agent. Wang et al. (ACL 2024) [PR]: "a single-agent LLM with strong prompts can achieve almost the same performance as the best existing discussion approach on a wide range of reasoning tasks and backbone LLMs"; multi-agent discussion performs better than a single agent only when there is no demonstration in the prompt. — [arXiv 2402.18272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18272), [ACL Anthology 2024.acl-long.331](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2024.acl-long.331)
- Broad debate re-evaluation. Zhang et al. (arXiv Feb 2025, v3 June 2025, position paper) [PP]: 5 multi-agent debate methods, 9 benchmarks, 4 foundation models; debate "often fails to outperform simple single-agent baselines such as Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency, even when consuming significantly more inference-time computation"; they blame limited benchmark coverage, weak baselines and inconsistent setups, and find model heterogeneity helps. — [arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)
- Smit et al. (arXiv Nov 2023) [PP as retrieved]: debating systems "in their current form, do not reliably outperform other proposed prompting strategies, such as self-consistency and ensembling using multiple reasoning paths". — [arXiv 2311.17371](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.17371v3)
- Same-model ensemble control. Li et al., "Rethinking Mixture-of-Agents" (arXiv Feb 2025; listed in TMLR 2026): Self-MoA, which aggregates several outputs of the single strongest model, beats standard Mixture-of-Agents that mixes six different models by 6.6% on AlpacaEval 2.0 and by 3.8% on average across MMLU, CRUX and MATH; mixing in weaker models lowers average proposer quality. (This partly conflicts with Zhang et al.'s claim that heterogeneity is a "universal antidote" for debate; the two study different architectures.) — [arXiv 2502.00674](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00674), [TMLR listing](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2026/li2026tmlr-rethinking/)
- Cost control. Kapoor et al., "AI Agents That Matter" (arXiv July 2024; TMLR 2025) [PR]: "'State-of-the-art' agent architectures for HumanEval do not outperform simple baselines". GPT-4 "warming" baseline 93.2% at $2.45 and "retry" 92.0% at $2.51, versus Reflexion 87.8% at $3.90, LATS 88.0% at $134.50 and LDB 91.0% at $2.19 (mean of 5 runs, 164 problems). — [arXiv 2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1)
- Equal-information control (opposite direction: reveals a real collaboration deficit). HiddenBench: 30.1% for teams with distributed information versus 80.7% for a single agent with all information. — [PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html)
- Trivial-agent control. The Agentic Benchmark Checklist paper [PP] found that in τ-bench a trivial agent returning empty responses succeeds on the 38% of airline tasks that are intentionally unsolvable, outperforming a GPT-4o-based agent, and that an agent could score 100% on SWE-Lancer by overwriting test files. — [arXiv 2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5)
- MARL analogue of a "no-coordination" control. Ellis et al., SMACv2 (NeurIPS 2023) [PR]: on the original SMAC, "an open-loop policy conditioned only on the timestep can achieve non-trivial win rates for many SMAC scenarios"; only 3s5z_vs_3s6z, corridor, 6h_vs_8z and 5m_vs_6m defeated it, so many headline results did not require reacting to teammates or observations at all. — [arXiv 2212.07489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07489)
- Self-play as an uncontrolled measure. Hanabi: SAD 23.97 self-play versus 2.52 cross-play. — [Hu et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979)

### Inferences
- A defensible control set for a team benchmark is therefore at least: (1) single agent, same total budget (tokens and tool calls), all services; (2) single agent, all information, unconstrained (ceiling); (3) team with communication disabled (shows whether the task needs coordination, the SMAC open-loop lesson); (4) trivial/no-op agent (shows floor and catches grader flaws); (5) same team, repeated seeds for variance.
- Most disappearing-gain cases come from short reasoning tasks where sampling diversity is the hidden mechanism. They do not prove teams are useless on tasks with genuine information or access partitioning; HiddenBench shows the control can cut the other way.

### Gaps
- I found no additional (beyond the already-covered studies) peer-reviewed case on long-horizon software tasks where a multi-agent gain disappeared specifically under an equal-token control.
- I did not find a study isolating a "no-communication team" control for LLM agents comparable to the SMAC open-loop analysis.

## 3. Multi-agent reinforcement learning: suites, unseen partners, fixed versus learned roles

### Takeaway
MARL's most transferable lesson is that self-play scores (a team evaluated with the partners it was built with) overstate coordination ability, and that suites moved toward held-out partner populations and randomised team composition. On roles, learned/emergent role methods (ROMA, RODE) beat non-role baselines on SMAC, but I found no head-to-head test against hand-assigned roles, so "emergent beats fixed" is not established there.

### Cited Findings
- Overcooked-AI. Carroll et al. (NeurIPS 2019) [PR]: self-play and population-based-training agents "perform very well when paired with themselves, but when paired with our human model, they are significantly worse than agents designed to play with the human model"; a user study with real humans "shows this pattern as well, though less strongly". Two-player, fully cooperative layouts. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2019/hash/f5b1b89d98b7286673128a5fb112cb9a-Abstract.html)
- Partner diversity. Strouse et al. (NeurIPS 2021) [PR]: "the crux of the problem is to produce a diverse set of training partners"; Fictitious Co-Play (best response to a population of self-play agents and their past checkpoints) scores significantly higher than self-play, population play and behavioural-cloning play with novel agent and human partners in Overcooked, and humans prefer it. — [arXiv 2110.08176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08176)
- Hanabi. Bard et al. (2020) [PR] pose two settings: self-play, and "ad-hoc team play", where score is measured when the agent "is paired with teammates chosen from a held-out pool of agents"; later work notes the ad-hoc protocol details were left open. — [arXiv 1902.00506](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00506v1), [Nekoei et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.10284)
- Zero-shot coordination. Hu et al. (ICML 2020) [PR] define it as "constructing AI agents that can coordinate with novel partners they have not seen before"; cross-play (pairing independently trained models) is "a cheap proxy". Hanabi Table 1: SAD cross-play 2.52 ± 0.34, self-play 23.97 ± 0.04; SAD+OP 15.32 ± 0.65 / 23.93; SAD+AUX+OP 22.07 ± 0.11 / 24.06. With humans, the other-play bot averaged 15.75 (45% bomb-out) versus 9.15 (85% bomb-out) for the self-play bot. The authors say other-play is not "a silver bullet". — [PMLR v119](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/hu20a.html), [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02979)
- Population methods without explicit diversity fail with unseen partners (Li et al. 2023, Overcooked). — [arXiv 2302.04831](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.04831)
- Melting Pot 2.0 (Agapiou et al., arXiv Nov 2022, v6 Oct 2023) [PP/tech report]: "an evaluation protocol that measures generalization to novel social partners in a set of canonical test scenarios"; each scenario pairs a "substrate" with "a reference set of co-players (a 'background population')"; version 2.0 adds "support for scenarios with asymmetric roles"; "a clear majority of scenarios in Melting Pot have mixed incentives". — [arXiv 2211.13746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13746)
- SMAC / SMACv2. SMACv2 adds procedural generation so that team compositions and start positions are randomised per episode, and requires generalisation "to previously unseen settings (from the same distribution) during evaluation". — [arXiv 2212.07489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07489)
- Google Research Football (Kurach et al., AAAI 2020) [PR]: full-game "Football Benchmarks" at three difficulties plus simpler "Football Academy" scenarios; supports multiplayer and multi-agent experiments. — [arXiv 1907.11180](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11180)
- Ad hoc teamwork. Mirsky et al. survey (EUMAS 2022) [PR] frames the problem as agents that "can work with new teammates without prior coordination". — [arXiv 2202.10450](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10450v2). N-agent ad hoc teamwork (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2024) [PR] generalises it: "a set of autonomous agents must interact with an uncontrolled set of teammates"; with one controlled agent it reduces to ad hoc teamwork; their method "enables out-of-distribution generalization to unseen teammates". — [arXiv 2404.10740](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10740v3)
- Emergent roles. ROMA (Wang et al., ICML 2020) [PR]: prior role-based methods "predefine role structures and behaviors" using domain knowledge; ROMA makes roles emerge via a stochastic role embedding with two regularisers; learned roles are "specialized, dynamic, and identifiable" and it advances state of the art on SMAC. — [arXiv 2003.08039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08039)
- RODE (Wang et al., ICLR 2021) [PR]: roles are discovered by clustering actions by their effects; a role selector re-assigns roles at a fixed interval; beats prior state of the art on 9 of 14 SMAC scenarios (10 of 14 in some summaries; abstract-level figure) and transfers to "new environments with three times the agents". — [arXiv 2010.01523](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01523), [ICLR 2021 page](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/poster/2717)

### Inferences
- A team benchmark that only evaluates homogeneous teams built by one developer is the analogue of self-play. Cross-play conditions (mixing models or scaffolds across services, or pairing a focal agent with a fixed background population of reference teammates, as Melting Pot does) test whether coordination relies on private conventions.
- Melting Pot's design is a precedent for "fix the test scenario and background partners, leave the focal agents' training/organisation free", i.e. prescribing the environment and partner set but not how the evaluated population organises itself.
- ROMA and RODE roles are dynamic (re-assigned within an episode) and were compared with role-free learners, not with fixed assigned roles. They support "role structure helps" more than "emergent beats assigned".
- Transfer caveat: these are RL agents trained over millions of episodes on tasks with dense symmetric structure. LLM agents communicate in natural language and are not trained jointly, so the convention-overfitting mechanism may be weaker; whether homogeneous LLM teams over-rely on shared priors is an open empirical question.

### Gaps
- Per-suite team sizes and role handling could not be verified from primary text for Melting Pot (number of substrates/scenarios, focal per-capita return metric) or Google Research Football (number of controlled players per scenario).
- Knott et al. 2021, "Evaluating the Robustness of Collaborative Agents" (unit-test style evaluation in Overcooked) was not retrievable this session; unverified lead.
- No source found that directly compares hand-assigned roles with learned roles on the same MARL benchmark.
- A standardised evaluation protocol for cooperative MARL (Gorsane et al. 2022, arXiv 2209.10485) is a relevant unverified lead on reporting practice.

## 4. Human team science: roles, size, transactive memory, hidden profiles, self-organisation

### Takeaway
Process loss with size is one of the most replicated findings in group research, synergy over the best member appears only on sufficiently complex or intellective tasks and at small sizes, and unshared information is systematically under-used. On structure, the better-supported claim is that some structure (clear specialisation, known "who knows what") helps even self-managed teams; I found no meta-analysis that directly compares assigned with emergent roles.

### Cited Findings
- Ringelmann / social loafing. Ingham et al. (1974) [PR] replicated declining individual output with group size from 1 to 6 and showed it also occurs in pseudo-groups (see section 1). — [paper PDF](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/jaro2020/PSX_512/um/Ingham_et_al_1974_Ringelmann_effect.pdf?lang=en). Karau and Williams (JPSP 1993) [PR], 78 studies: social loafing is "moderate in magnitude and generalizable across tasks and subject populations", larger when contributions cannot be evaluated, the task feels unimportant, or the group is large. — [typeset.io copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/social-loafing-a-meta-analytic-review-and-theoretical-22g4ic0jro.pdf) (via secondary summary)
- Steiner's process loss and task types: see section 1. — [Steiner's taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner%27s_Taxonomy_of_Tasks)
- Size and synergy. Laughlin et al. 2006: groups of 3–5 beat the best of an equal number of individuals; pairs do not. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16649860/). Almaatouq et al. 2021: synergy appears for complex tasks only. — [PMC8433503](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8433503)
- Team-size meta-analysis exists: Bernerth, Beus, Helmuth and Boyd, "The more the merrier or too many cooks spoil the pot?", Journal of Organizational Behavior 44(8), 2023, pp. 1230–1262 [PR]; I could not retrieve its effect sizes. — [catalogue record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/the-more-the-merrier-or-too-many-cooks-spoil-the-pot-a-meta-analytic-examination-of-team-size-and-team-effectiveness-bernerth-jeremy/10014456772)
- Collective intelligence. Riedl et al. (PNAS 2021) [PR], 22 studies, 1,356 groups: support for a general collective intelligence factor; "group collaboration process is more important in predicting CI than the skill of individual members". Contested: some argue individual intelligence is the only real predictor, and a 2025 preprint by Kommol, Riedl and Woolley proposes a three-factor structure instead. — [PMC8166150](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8166150), [CMU release](https://www.cmu.edu/tepper/news/stories/2021/may/collective-intelligence-research.html)
- Hidden profiles. Meta-analysis of 65 studies (101 effects, 3,189 groups): two SD more discussion of common than unique information; hidden-profile groups far less likely to find the correct solution than full-information groups. — [doi 10.1177/1088868311417243](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1088868311417243)
- Information sharing. Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch (JAP 2009) [PR], 72 independent studies (4,795 groups; N = 17,279): information sharing predicts team performance across all moderator levels; sharing of unique information ("uniqueness") and "openness" have different effects; sharing is enhanced by task demonstrability, discussion structure and cooperation, and reduced by information distribution, informational interdependence and member heterogeneity, i.e. teams share least exactly when it matters most. — [UCF record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/12034), [preprint](https://atlas.northwestern.edu/papers/information.pdf), [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/03/090323101821.htm)
- Transactive memory. Bachrach et al. (JAP 2019, 104(3), 464–493) [PR], 76 studies, 6,869 sampling units: TMS relates positively to team performance, moderated by national culture; environmental volatility, leadership effectiveness and team human capital are positively associated with TMS; informational and gender diversity negatively. — [SKKU record](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/transactive-memory-systems-in-context-a-meta-analytic-examination/). A later lab-study meta-analysis (Fausett et al.) reports pooled TMS–outcome r = .44, 95% CI [0.36, 0.50]. — [SJSU record](https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/6943). Zhou and Pazos (2020) find TMS more strongly linked to affective than performance outcomes. — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/TPM-05-2020-0036/full/html)
- Structure inside self-managed teams. Bunderson and Boumgarden (Organization Science 21(3), 2010) [PR], self-managed production teams at a Fortune 100 high-technology firm: more team structure (specialisation, hierarchy, formalisation) was associated with more learning via psychological safety, information sharing and less conflict, and with productivity gains in a subset of teams; title claim: "Why 'bureaucratic' teams can be better learners". — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/orsc.1090.0483). Bresman and Zellmer-Bruhn (Organization Science 2013) [PR]: in self-managed pharmaceutical R&D teams, team-level structure was tied to more learning but organisation-level structure to less. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ororsc/v24y2013i4p1120-1139.html)
- Shared (largely emergent) leadership. Meta-analysis of 50 effect sizes (team n = 3,198): positive relationship between shared leadership and team performance. — [Journal of Management, doi 10.1177/0149206314525205](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0149206314525205)
- Planning. Bayesian meta-analysis (33 samples, 1,885 teams): team planning–performance ρ = .31, stronger for less interdependent tasks and less specialised members; literature "severely underpowered". — [PMC9838875](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9838875/)
- Self-managing teams. A systematic review of 84 studies concludes self-managing teams benefit performance only if members have the competencies to operate in a self-managing system. — [Heidelberg record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1833288076). A qualitative review of 28 studies found only job satisfaction consistently related to self-managing teamwork. — [record](https://www.pollux-fid.de/r/cr-10.1177/1059601103257989)

### Inferences
- The human evidence favours a contingency view: self-organisation works when members are competent and the task is complex and non-routine; structure (clear specialisation, a known directory of who owns what) helps learning and information flow even when authority is decentralised. The Bunderson/Bresman pair suggests structure that defines roles within the team helps while structure imposed from above on how the team interacts externally can hurt.
- "Assigned ownership" in the benchmark is functionally a pre-built transactive memory system (everyone knows who owns which service). The TMS literature predicts this should help, and the hidden-profile literature predicts that agents will still under-share what only they know, so the benchmark should measure whether owner-only facts reach the agents who need them.
- Size findings (3–5 for synergy on intellective tasks; losses growing with size) are from humans, where motivation loss is a major mechanism. LLM agents may not "loaf" for the same reasons, so only the coordination-loss component should be assumed to transfer.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis directly comparing assigned with emergent roles on team performance was found.
- The "two-pizza rule" has no empirical study that I could find; it should be treated as practitioner heuristic. Hackman and Vidmar's (1970) "4.6" optimal size appeared only in a blog ([Mountain Goat Software](https://mountaingoatsoftware.com/blog/the-just-right-size-for-agile-teams)).
- Bernerth et al. (2023) effect sizes, Stewart (2006, Journal of Management 32(1), 29–55) meta-analysis of team design features, and Staats, Milkman and Fox's "team scaling fallacy" were identified but their findings not retrieved.

## 5. Software organisations: Conway's law, congruence, team topologies, ownership

### Takeaway
Mirroring between organisation and architecture is common and empirically supported inside firms, but it is not universal and notably did not hold in open collaborative (mostly open-source software) projects. Ownership concentration predicts quality in large commercial codebases (Microsoft) but the effect is weak or absent in several open-source and small-company replications; Brooks's law evidence in open source is genuinely contested.

### Cited Findings
- Mirroring test. MacCormack, Rusnak and Baldwin (Research Policy 41(8), 2012, 1309–1324) [PR]: matched-pair products; "loosely coupled organizations will develop more modular designs than tightly coupled organizations"; they "find strong evidence to support the mirroring hypothesis". — [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/34403525)
- Mirroring review. Colfer and Baldwin (Industrial and Corporate Change 25(5), 2016, 709–738) [PR], 142 empirical studies: mirroring is common in industry and firm studies but not universal; in technologically dynamic industries "partial mirroring, where knowledge boundaries are drawn more broadly than operational boundaries" is likely the better strategy; firms can "break the mirror" via modular partitions within their boundaries or relational contracts across them; studies of open collaborative projects, mostly software, did not support the hypothesis, attributed to digital tools enabling other coordination modes. — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/indcch/v25y2016i5p709-738..html), [Harvard DASH PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/33785675/1/colfer,baldwin_mirroring-hypothesis.pdf)
- Organisational structure and quality. Nagappan, Murphy and Basili (ICSE 2008) [PR], Windows Vista: organisational-structure metrics were statistically significant predictors of failure-proneness, with precision and recall "significantly higher than using traditional metrics like churn, complexity, coverage, dependencies, and pre-release bug measures". Secondary slides give 86.2% precision / 84.0% recall for organisational structure versus 78.6% / 79.9% for churn; a blog reports a replication with lower predictive value. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=153188), [paper PDF](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/basili/publications/proceedings/P125.pdf)
- Ownership. Bird et al., "Don't Touch My Code!" (ESEC/FSE 2011) [PR], Windows Vista and Windows 7: ownership measures, including number of low-expertise ("minor") contributors, relate to pre-release faults and post-release failures. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=161465)
- Open-source replication, contested. Foucault et al. (EASE 2014) [PR], seven Java open-source projects: the ownership–fault relationship was weak (at best under half of projects significant, at worst none); module size mattered more; attributed to contribution distributions and "heroes". — [paper page](https://se.labri.fr/a/EASE14-foucault/). A later version confirms a relationship but with low importance relative to lines of code. — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01433069/file/main.pdf)
- Greiler, Herzig and Czerwonka (MSR 2015) [PR], four Microsoft products: "confirm the original findings by Bird et al. that code ownership correlates with code quality"; classifier median precision 0.74 / recall 0.38 at file level and 0.76 / 0.60 at directory level. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/code-ownership-and-software-quality-a-replication-study/)
- A 2024 literature review plus replication on a company codebase found neither code metrics nor ownership metrics significantly correlated with bug counts. — [arXiv 2405.15665](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15665) [PP]
- Team size in open source, contested. Scholtes et al. (Empirical Software Engineering 2016) [PR], 58 projects, over 580,000 commits, over 30,000 developers: per-developer productivity decreases as team size grows. Sornette et al. (2014) reported superlinear scaling of production with team size; Maillart and Sornette (2016) argue the conflict is about production versus productivity — [arXiv 1608.03608](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.03608). Gote et al. (2022) on a large GitHub dataset find a negative relationship "in all cases" — [arXiv 2201.04588](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.04588). A 2024 reanalysis attributes the divergence to selection and instrumentation biases (e.g. 81.7% of Sornette's projects had fewer than 50 contributors, Scholtes sampled larger ones) — [arXiv 2408.04782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04782v1) [PP]. Schweik and English found on SourceForge that more developers go with a higher likelihood of project success, "favor[ing] Linus' Law over Brooks' Law" (outcome is success, not schedule) — [record](https://works.bepress.com/charles_schweik/11).
- Loosely coupled teams. DORA [Industry, survey-based, correlational]: organisational and technical structures are "predictors for achieving continuous delivery"; loosely coupled means teams can test and deploy without depending on other teams; warns that many nominally service-oriented architectures do not permit independent testing and deployment. — [DORA capability page](https://dora.dev/capabilities/loosely-coupled-teams/), [2022 report](https://dora.dev/research/2022/dora-report/2022-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf)
- Inverse Conway manoeuvre is a practitioner recommendation (Thoughtworks Technology Radar, 2014) with no measured evidence found. — [Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/inverse-conway-maneuver)

### Inferences
- "One agent per service" is a fully mirrored organisation. The literature says this minimises needed communication for changes inside a service but makes cross-service changes the place where coordination requirements exceed default communication paths, which is where quality problems concentrated in the congruence studies. A benchmark's most diagnostic tasks are therefore those that cut across ownership boundaries (contract or event-schema changes).
- Colfer and Baldwin's "partial mirroring" (knowledge boundaries broader than operational boundaries) maps onto a design choice: owners can be allowed to read other services' contracts or code but only write their own. This is a testable condition.
- The assigned-versus-emergent ownership question is not settled by this literature. Strong ownership correlates with fewer defects in commercial settings with assigned ownership; open-source projects, where ownership is more emergent, show weak ownership effects and also break the mirror. These are observational and confounded by process differences.
- All of this is human evidence. LLM agents can read an entire codebase quickly and have no persistent expertise unless given memory, so the "minor contributor lacks context" mechanism behind ownership effects may be weaker or different.

### Gaps
- Colfer and Baldwin's per-setting support percentages were not retrievable (HBS page returned 403); I recall figures of roughly 70% support within firms and lower elsewhere but could not verify them.
- No academic empirical evaluation of Team Topologies (Skelton and Pais, 2019) was found; treat as practitioner framework.
- No study was found that directly tests Brooks's original claim (adding people to an already-late project) rather than size versus productivity.
- Mockus/Herbsleb-style work on implicit ownership in Apache was not retrieved.

## 6. Benchmark design guidance from validity work on agents (2024–2026)

### Takeaway
The validity literature converges on a short list that applies directly to multi-agent evaluation: report cost and plot accuracy against it, state whether the benchmark measures the model, the scaffold or both, include trivial-agent and human baselines, report statistical uncertainty, and check that graders cannot be passed without the capability. None of the checklists found addresses multi-agent systems specifically.

### Cited Findings
- AI Agents That Matter (Kapoor, Stroebl, Siegel, Nadgir, Narayanan; arXiv July 2024; TMLR 2025) [PR]: "agent evaluations must be cost-controlled"; plot a Pareto frontier of accuracy against dollar cost; joint optimisation "leads to 53% lower variable cost with similar accuracy" on HotPotQA; "the difference between model evaluation and downstream evaluation is underappreciated" and "downstream evaluation benchmarks must be separate from, or at least variants of, model evaluation benchmarks"; holdout sets should match the claimed level of generality, and "the majority of benchmarks do not include an appropriate held-out set" (17 surveyed); evaluation scripts often assume a particular agent design and "there are no clear standards for providing agent evaluation scripts". — [arXiv 2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1), [TMLR listing](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2025/kapoor2025tmlr-ai/)
- Agentic Benchmark Checklist (Zhu et al., arXiv July 2025, v5 Aug 2025) [PP]. Definitions: outcome validity, "the evaluation result (e.g., tests or checks) truly indicates task success"; task validity, "a task should be solvable if and only if the agent possesses the target capability". Of 10 benchmarks assessed, 7 had outcome-validity flaws, 7 had task-validity issues and all 10 had reporting limitations. Flaws can cause under- or over-estimation "by up to 100% in relative terms". Examples: SWE-bench Verified tests miss incorrect patches (24% of top-50 leaderboard positions affected); KernelBench overestimates by about 31% absolute; OSWorld underestimates by 28% absolute because 13 of 46 Chrome tasks broke; CVE-Bench overestimation reduced by 33% after applying the checklist. Reporting items include: R.5 construct validity (capability clearly specified), R.6 state whether the benchmark evaluates the model, the agent framework or both, R.10 statistical significance, R.12 non-AI or human baselines, R.13 results for trivial agents, plus open data and harness, contamination measures and flaw-impact analysis. The paper does not discuss multi-agent systems. — [arXiv 2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5)
- Construct validity review. Bean et al., "Measuring what Matters" (NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks) [PR]: systematic review of 445 LLM benchmarks by 29 reviewers; only 16% used statistical tests; about half measure abstract phenomena without clear, uncontested definitions; 38% reuse existing benchmarks or exams. Eight recommendations include: define the phenomenon, measure it and only it, build a representative dataset, acknowledge limits of reused data, prepare for contamination, use statistical methods to compare models, and conduct error analysis. — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1967e0fc3aa6cbbace562f5cb8e3954e-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html)
- Holistic Agent Leaderboard (Kapoor, Stroebl et al., arXiv Oct 2025; ICLR 2026) [PR]: 21,730 agent rollouts across 9 models and 9 benchmarks, about $40,000; evaluates along three axes (scaffold, model, benchmark) to expose interactions single-axis leaderboards hide; higher reasoning effort reduced accuracy in most runs; LLM-aided log inspection found shortcut behaviours such as agents searching HuggingFace for the benchmark rather than solving the task. — [arXiv 2510.11977](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11977), [ICLR 2026 proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/a0928f924a344aaebbb7f6cd8d56e34c-Abstract-Conference.html)
- From MARL: SMACv2's diagnosis that a benchmark can be solved without the capability it claims to test (open-loop policies), and the fix of procedurally randomising composition and start conditions. — [arXiv 2212.07489](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.07489)
- From the debate literature: weak baselines, narrow benchmark coverage and inconsistent setups are named as the reasons multi-agent debate looked better than it is. — [arXiv 2502.08788](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)

### Inferences
- Applied to a multi-agent benchmark, the scaffold/model distinction gains a third layer: model, per-agent scaffold, and team organisation (roles, topology, communication protocol). Following HAL and R.6, results should say which of these were held fixed and which varied.
- Task validity for a collaboration benchmark means a task should be solvable if and only if agents coordinate. The SMAC open-loop result and the equal-information control are the direct tests: if a no-communication team or a single service owner can pass, the task does not measure coordination.
- Cost reporting for teams should be total tokens and dollars across all agents, plus wall-clock, since parallel teams trade cost for time; this trade-off is invisible if only accuracy is reported.
- Log inspection (HAL) and failure taxonomies (MAST) are complementary to scores; given that automated blame attribution is only about 53% accurate at agent level (Who&When), per-agent blame from an LLM judge should not be a headline metric.

### Gaps
- No checklist or validity paper specific to multi-agent benchmarks was found; the guidance above is transferred from single-agent work.
- The final third of the ABC paper and of AI Agents That Matter were not read (page truncation).

## 7. Is there an argued position on prescribing roles and team size versus leaving them free?

### Takeaway
Yes, but the positions are scattered and the direct LLM evidence is thin: one large 2026 preprint argues for minimal structure with capable models, while fault-tolerance and human-team evidence favour some imposed structure, and several sources suggest the answer depends on agent capability. The evidence most consistent with all of this is to treat structure as an experimental factor and run prescribed and free conditions separately.

### Cited Findings

**For leaving structure free (or minimal)**
- Dochkina, "Drop the Hierarchy and Roles" (arXiv March 2026; submitted to IEEE Access, acceptance not shown; single author) [PP]: 25,000 tasks, 8 models, 4–256 agents, 8 coordination protocols from imposed hierarchy to self-organisation. A hybrid "Sequential" protocol (fixed agent order, self-selected roles) outperforms centralised coordination by 14% (p < 0.001); quality spread between protocols is 44% (Cohen's d = 1.86); scaling to 256 agents without quality degradation (p = 0.61); 5,006 unique roles emerged from 8 agents. Conclusion: "give agents a mission, a protocol, and a capable model -- not a pre-assigned role." — [arXiv 2603.28990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28990)
- MARL: ROMA argues predefined roles require domain knowledge and that emergent roles can be "specialized, dynamic, and identifiable"; RODE's learned roles transfer to three times as many agents. — [arXiv 2003.08039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08039), [arXiv 2010.01523](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01523)
- Open collaborative software projects operate without mirroring formal structure (Colfer and Baldwin). — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/indcch/v25y2016i5p709-738..html)
- Shared, emergent leadership correlates positively with team performance in humans. — [doi 10.1177/0149206314525205](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0149206314525205)

**For prescribing structure**
- The same Dochkina preprint reports a capability threshold: strong models self-organise effectively, while models below the threshold still benefit from rigid structure; and its best protocol was a hybrid with an imposed order, not full autonomy. — [arXiv 2603.28990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.28990)
- Hierarchical structure is the most fault-tolerant in Huang et al. (5.5% drop versus 10.5% and 23.7%). — [arXiv 2408.00989](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00989v4)
- A lightweight imposed discussion protocol substantially narrows the distributed-information gap in HiddenBench; left alone, LLM groups reproduce conformity and shared-information bias. — [PMLR v306](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html), [arXiv 2505.11556v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11556v2)
- In human self-managed teams, more team-level structure was associated with more learning and productivity (Bunderson and Boumgarden 2010); self-managing teams help only when members have the competence for it. — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/orsc.1090.0483), [Heidelberg record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1833288076)
- Discussion structure is one of the factors that increases information sharing in human teams (Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch). — [UCF record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/12034)
- MAST attributes many failures to system design (specification and inter-agent misalignment), implying design of the organisation matters. — [arXiv 2503.13657](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657v2)

**For treating structure as an experimental factor / reporting both**
- HAL's three-axis design (scaffold × model × benchmark) is an explicit argument that holding one axis fixed hides interactions. — [arXiv 2510.11977](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11977)
- ABC item R.6 asks benchmarks to state whether they evaluate the model, the agent framework or both. — [arXiv 2507.02825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5)
- AI Agents That Matter: model evaluation and downstream (system) evaluation answer different questions and should be separate benchmarks or variants. — [arXiv 2407.01502](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1)
- MultiAgentBench varies topology (star, chain, tree, graph) as a factor within one benchmark and finds the ranking is scenario-dependent. — [arXiv 2503.01935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01935)
- Melting Pot fixes scenarios and background partners while leaving the evaluated population's construction free; Hanabi reports self-play and ad-hoc team play as two settings. — [arXiv 2211.13746](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13746), [arXiv 1902.00506](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00506v1)
- Human contingency evidence: whether teams beat individuals flips with task complexity (Almaatouq et al.), so any single structural condition will generalise poorly. — [PMC8433503](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8433503)

### Inferences
- Prescribing roles and size makes the benchmark a test of model ability within a fixed organisation (comparable across models, the "model evaluation" reading). Leaving them free makes it a test of the whole system including organisational design (the "downstream" reading), and confounds model ability with design skill unless organisation is logged and reported. These are different constructs; the validity literature's advice is to name which one is being measured rather than to pick one universally.
- A two-track design follows naturally: a prescribed track (fixed one-owner-per-service, fixed team size, fixed communication channel) for model comparison, and an open track (agents or developers choose division of labour, possibly including collapsing to one agent) for system comparison, with the same tasks, budget accounting and controls. I found no paper that states this two-track recommendation in exactly these terms for multi-agent benchmarks; it is a synthesis of HAL, AI Agents That Matter, MultiAgentBench and Melting Pot practice.
- The capability-threshold result, if it replicates, predicts that a prescribed-structure benchmark will increasingly understate what strong models can do when self-organising, and that a free-structure benchmark will penalise weaker models; reporting both conditions gives a measurable "structure benefit" (score prescribed minus score free) per model.
- Caution: the strongest pro-emergence LLM result is a single-author preprint not yet peer-reviewed, with abstract-level numbers only; the pro-structure human findings are observational; the MARL role results concern jointly trained RL agents. None is decisive for LLM service-owner teams.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed LLM study was found that holds tasks, models and budget fixed while comparing assigned ownership with self-organised ownership on software-engineering tasks.
- A second 2026 preprint on spontaneous role differentiation ("SwarmWorld", reported as arXiv 2608.26081) surfaced in search but could not be verified.
- No source was found arguing explicitly that a benchmark should prescribe team size; arguments found concern roles, topology and protocol.
