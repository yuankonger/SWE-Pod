# Prescribe ownership, then let models organise

SeedCo should run both conditions, as separate tracks that answer different questions: a prescribed track (the evaluator fixes who owns which services, how many agents there are and the message channel) as the headline and the training target, and a model-chosen track (a lead agent decides how many agents to spawn and which services each holds) as a diagnostic, with tasks, hidden checks, model, tools and the total token budget identical across both. The evidence does not support picking one. Prescribing structure measures how well a model works inside a given organisation and is what almost every benchmark does; leaving structure free measures whether the model can design an organisation, which is what shipped products now do and what three 2026 orchestration benchmarks score. No published study compares the two on repository-scale software work at a matched budget, and the nearest evidence points both ways: strong models gain a few percent from choosing their own roles, weaker models lose, fully unconstrained teams are the worst protocol tested, and models left free both over-spawn and under-delegate. Across roughly seventy benchmarks reviewed, most fix a team of two or a game-defined handful, prescribe roles, and omit a solo agent given the same total budget; where a solo reference exists, teams usually cost 1.4 to 5 times more and match or trail it. SeedCo's existing three conditions already supply the two controls the field most often lacks (an equal-budget solo agent and a no-messaging team), and they bracket the model-chosen track: solo is the one-agent endpoint and one owner per service is the other. What to prescribe is ownership, count and channel, not job titles or personas, because access-based roles change outcomes and expert labels do not. The gaps SeedCo can fill are specific: no benchmark has agents owning separate services joined by contracts, none partitions read access to code, none holds total tokens equal between a team and a solo agent on a purpose-built team coding task, and none runs prescribed and model-chosen structure side by side.

## Seventy benchmarks, mostly fixed teams without an equal-budget solo

The field divides into three groups: benchmarks where several agents write code or do workplace tasks, general coordination benchmarks built on puzzles, games and embodied tasks, and a small 2026 group that scores how a lead model organises sub-agents. The tables give the setup of the main entries. Most 2026 items are preprints, several were read only from abstracts, and model names are reproduced as the sources give them.

### Coding and workplace benchmarks prescribe ownership, not job titles

Only four benchmarks define tasks whose structure requires more than one agent editing code: CooperBench, ParallACT, NP-Bench and AsynCodeBench. The rest are single-agent task sets on which a team is optionally run (SWE-bench, Commit0, SRDD and similar), or workplace simulations with one evaluated agent.

Three design choices recur. **Benchmarks built around parallel work prescribe ownership** (a requirement, a file scope, a producer or consumer side), while job titles such as manager, coder and reviewer belong to the systems being evaluated; EntCollabBench and MultiAgentBench are the exceptions. **Real information partition is rare**: ParallACT and CooperBench withhold the partner's requirement, NP-Bench assigns write scopes, EntCollabBench isolates tools, and no coding benchmark found stops an agent reading another owner's source ([ParallACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314), [NP-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07261), [EntCollabBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.08761v1)). **Cost is reported but not bounded**: the only bounds found are a 200-step cap and an equal time budget, and no purpose-built team coding benchmark fixes a token budget across solo and team conditions ([E2EDevBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04064), [concurrency study](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10263v1)). Two datasets round out the picture without scoring a team: AgenticFlict found merge conflicts in 27.67% of processed agent-written pull requests, and BulkPR-Bench has one agent order a queue of 581 interacting pull requests ([AgenticFlict](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03551), [BulkPR-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02685)).

Workplace benchmarks outside this table are single-agent. GDPval, the Remote Labor Index and APEX-Agents are all one-shot tasks "performed in isolation from any broader business context" ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/blog/what-do-economic-value-benchmarks-tell-us)). Vending-Bench Arena places several models at one location but scores each individually, and Anthropic's Project Vend phase two assigned its shopkeeper, chief executive and merchandise agents by hand ([Andon Labs](https://andonlabs.com/evals/vending-bench-2), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-2)).

Baselines are where this group is weakest. Cross-model comparison is universal; a single agent with all information appears in Silo-Bench, RoCoBench, PARTNR, AgentWorld and HiddenBench; a no-communication team appears in AgentWorld, GovSim, AvalonBench, BattleAgentBench and Welfare Diplomacy; and no non-coding benchmark found gives the solo agent the team's total token budget ([Silo-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01045v1), [AgentWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31590v1), [GovSim](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16698)). AgentWorld's ladder (random, single, no communication, shared plan, team, oracle communication) is the most complete in one benchmark. Random and do-nothing floors earn their place: some reasoning models score below random on COMMA, and every framework tested on two CREW-Wildfire levels often scored below a do-nothing baseline ([COMMA](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07553), [CREW-Wildfire](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.05178)).

### Orchestration benchmarks leave structure free and fix resources

A third group, all from 2026, starts from the opposite premise: the model under test builds the team.

| Benchmark | Setup | What the model decides | What the evaluator fixes | Scoring |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [SwarmBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30661v1) (EMNLP 2026 Findings) | 8 tasks of 50 examples covering decomposition, delegation and aggregation | Sub-agent count, roles and backbone models, created at run time | A fixed model pool; the lead cannot call tools or answer alone | Accuracy, parallelism rate and gain, dollars, judge-rated process quality |
| [ClawArena-Team](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.31174.md) (Jun 2026) | 41 multi-turn workspace scenarios, 258 rounds | Each sub-agent's prompt, model, tool subset and path whitelist | A fixed sub-agent pool; the lead reaches part of the workspace only through sub-agents | Task completion multiplied by permission and routing precision; no model judge |
| [DecisionBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19099) (May 2026) | 23,375 instances on GAIA, tau-bench and BFCL | Whether to delegate and to which of 11 peer models | The peer pool and awareness conditions | Quality, cost, delegation rate, routing fidelity, a perfect-routing ceiling |
| [MAS-Orchestra's MasBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14652) (Jan 2026) | Tasks controlled along depth, horizon, breadth, parallelism and robustness | The whole system, generated in one step | The allowed "degree" (at most one sub-agent, or unconstrained) as a variable | Accuracy by task axis |

The shared convention is to fix what makes scores comparable and free what is being measured, and to report process metrics beside the outcome. **None of these four fixes the number of agents**, and none targets multi-service software work with execution-based scoring of both outcome and delegation.

## Where a solo reference exists, teams cost more and rarely win

The results matter for the design question because they set how large an effect of structure can be. On ParallACT, Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.8 passes **62% of contradictory and 86% of complementary** instances as a single agent holding both requirements, against **16% and 57%** for two unaware parallel agents; messaging lifts that only to 20% and 63%, and running the two agents in sequence recovers most of the loss, which ties the deficit to concurrency and not to splitting requirements ([ParallACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314)). CooperBench reports two agents retaining 0.59 of pooled solo success, with post-merge success of **17.14% with messaging against 17.64% without** ([CooperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13295)). Turning on native sub-agents moved SWE-bench Verified pass rates from 83.0% to 59.0% for Claude Code and left Codex unchanged at 71.0%, while using 1.41 to 3.31 times the tokens; a third of failure patterns involved shared state and merging ([concurrency study](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10263v1)). TeamBench's mean uplift from a three-role team was +0.5 points (p = 0.20), positive for weaker models and negative for one open-weight model ([TeamBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07073)). Five of six multi-agent workflows in BenchAgent trailed the single agent by 1.71 to 8.76 points under shared step limits ([BenchAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05670)).

The distributed-information benchmarks show the other face of the same result. HiddenBench teams reach **30.1%** where a single agent with everything reaches **80.7%**, and a lightweight imposed discussion protocol narrows the gap substantially ([HiddenBench](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html)). Silo-Bench agents gather the pieces but fail to combine them: at the easiest level partial correctness is 88.0% and success 62.0% ([Silo-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01045v1)). Adding agents mostly hurts once they interact: Silo-Bench success falls from 61.2% with two agents to 18.1% with a hundred, MineCollab cooking and crafting fall from as high as 90% to under 30% between two and five agents, and LLM-Deliberation agreement falls from 90% with three parties to 33% with six ([Silo-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01045v1), [MineCollab](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17950), [LLM-Deliberation](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.17234)).

Teams do win in identifiable circumstances. AgentWorld's teams reach 52.0% against 28.6% for a single agent on tasks built around asymmetric abilities, though without budget control ([AgentWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31590v1)). A Google and MIT study of 260 configurations found effects from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to -70.0% on sequential planning ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). Systems that add a coordination mechanism report gains, such as NP-Bench's planner taking clean-merge success from 1 of 9 to 9 of 9 scripted scenarios and DeLM's shared log lifting Terminal-Bench scores, but each is reported by the authors of the winning system ([NP-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07261), [DeLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.10662)). The implication for SeedCo is that differences between two ways of choosing a structure will be smaller than the team-versus-solo gap and will need tight budget control and repeated runs to detect.

## Free structure exposes organising skill; prescription keeps scores comparable

### The case for letting the model decide

The strongest argument is about construct. A benchmark that fixes structure and scores only the final output cannot see organisational skill. In ClawArena-Team, **cost varies more than 100-fold across twelve lead models while the management score varies less than 4-fold**, and in DecisionBench end-task quality is statistically flat across conditions while delegation rates move from 7.5% to 29.5% and perfect routing would add 15 to 31 points ([ClawArena-Team](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.31174.md), [DecisionBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19099)). Organisation is where models differ, and outcome scores under a fixed roster hide it.

The second argument is realism. Shipped products let the lead choose: Claude Code "decides the number of teammates to spawn based on your task", a coordinator Devin splits work among child sessions, and OpenAI's multi-agent mode lets the lead spawn sub-agents under a concurrency cap ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams), [Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/devin-can-now-manage-devins), [Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blog/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench)). A benchmark with only a fixed roster does not test the configuration people deploy.

The third is direct experimental evidence, which is thin. A single-author preprint by Dochkina, scored by a model judge on synthetic tasks, found a protocol with a fixed turn order and self-selected roles beat a central coordinator by 12 to 14% across three models, and Claude Sonnet 4.6 did 3.5% better with self-chosen than with assigned roles ([Dochkina](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28990v1)). Captain Agent, which re-forms its team between rounds, beat a team built once in four of five scenarios in its authors' own ablation, at the highest cost ([Captain Agent](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.19425)). In reinforcement learning, methods that let roles emerge (ROMA, RODE) beat role-free learners, though no source compares them with hand-assigned roles ([ROMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08039), [RODE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01523)). Hand-written "role assignment" baselines also sit within noise of plain chain-of-thought in comparison tables ([ADAS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.08435)).

### The case for telling the model its team

The first argument is comparability. If each model picks its own team, a score mixes coding ability, coordination ability and design skill, and two models' results differ in an uncontrolled variable. The validity literature asks benchmarks to state whether they evaluate the model, the framework or both, and argues that model evaluation and system evaluation should be separate benchmarks or variants ([Agentic Benchmark Checklist](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825v5), [AI Agents That Matter](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1)). Even the free-structure benchmarks concede the point by fixing the sub-agent pool, which SwarmBench's authors say "improves comparability but may limit the generality of the conclusions" ([SwarmBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30661v1)).

The second is that models organise badly when left alone, in both directions. Anthropic's early research system spawned "50 subagents for simple queries" until explicit scaling rules went into the lead's prompt ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Reasoning-model orchestrators in MAS-Orchestra "often solve the task first and then delegate to only one simple sub-agent", and Claude Code's documentation lists the lead implementing tasks itself and stopping early as known behaviours ([MAS-Orchestra](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14652), [Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). In every vendor system examined the fix was a prompt heuristic for team size, which is a partial return to prescription.

The third is head-to-head evidence against free structure. Dochkina's fully autonomous protocol was the worst of four (0.503 against 0.724 for the hybrid), and a weaker model scored 9.6% lower with self-chosen roles ([Dochkina](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28990v1)). A Salesforce-led study reports that automatically generated systems underperform self-consistency at up to ten times the cost, and that expert-designed systems beat automatic ones on a dataset built so that structure matters ([Jwalapuram et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13003)). Self-organising teams fail to match their best member even when told who the expert is, losing up to 41.1%, and the loss grows with team size ([Pappu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01011)). Cursor reported that twenty flat self-coordinating agents slowed "to the effective throughput of two or three" until a planner, worker and judge hierarchy was imposed, while Anthropic's sixteen-agent compiler build worked without an orchestrator only after a human repartitioned the task ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents), [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). Both are uncontrolled vendor accounts.

Human evidence leans the same way without settling it. In self-managed production teams, more team-level structure went with more learning, and structured discussion is one of the factors that raises information sharing ([Bunderson and Boumgarden](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/orsc.1090.0483), [Mesmer-Magnus and DeChurch](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/12034)). Hierarchical agent systems lost least (5.5%) when one agent was made faulty ([Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00989v4)). No meta-analysis comparing assigned with emergent roles was found.

### What kind of prescription matters

The evidence separates two things called "roles". A role that changes what an agent may touch and see is a structural variable: removing role assignments cut ChatDev's executability from 0.88 to 0.58, and removing any one Magentic-One agent cost 21 to 39% ([ChatDev](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.07924), [Magentic-One](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04468)). A persona that only labels an agent an expert is a prompt-style variable: across 162 personas and four model families, adding one "does not improve model performance" ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054)). Enforcement matters for behaviour more than for score: in TeamBench, prompt-only and container-enforced roles gave 42.7% and 40.5%, but verifier attempts to edit code fell from 256 to 72 under enforcement ([TeamBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07073)). The useful prescription for a benchmark is therefore who owns what, enforced by the sandbox, not job titles.

What is missing is the decisive study. No paper holds tasks, models and budget fixed while comparing assigned with self-organised ownership on software work, and no paper argues head-on that benchmarks should fix or free team structure. The recommendation to run both is a synthesis of the three-axis leaderboard argument, the model-versus-system distinction and the fixed-scenario, free-population practice of Melting Pot and Hanabi's separate self-play and ad hoc settings ([Holistic Agent Leaderboard](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.11977), [Melting Pot 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13746), [Hanabi challenge](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00506v1)).

## Labs report both; evaluators cap the team and observe it

Anthropic is the only lab found that publishes multi-agent evaluation method in detail, and it runs both designs side by side. The Claude Opus 5 system card uses a peer team of **N = 5 or 10 fixed by the evaluator**, where all agents have identical tools and see the full task, and an asynchronous sub-agent harness with "no cap on the number of subagents", where each sub-agent sees only the lead's instructions. On BrowseComp the ten-agent team reached 93.6%, 3.1 points above the best single agent, with a 5.9-fold latency speedup; on ProgramBench a five-agent team reached the single agent's score 2.2 times faster. The comparison is not token-matched: every agent has a 1M-token limit, so teams get more in total, and the card plots score against tokens and cost so the reader can do the matching ([Claude Opus 5 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/b514064af1408018e64b1ad24e7d5e75850b4ffd/Claude+Opus+5+System+Card.pdf)). The same card records a reviewer's finding that the alignment audit had limited coverage of multi-agent settings and that the model "can relay claims from subagents to users without verifying them".

Other labs publish less. Microsoft's Magentic-One fixed five agents and ablated them, with no cost measurement ([Magentic-One](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04468)). Cognition moved from "Don't Build Multi-Agents" in June 2025 to managed child sessions and a list of working patterns in 2026 without publishing an evaluation ([Cognition 2025](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents), [Cognition 2026](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)). No first-party evaluation of multi-agent mode was found for OpenAI or Google products, and nothing was found for Meta.

Independent evaluators mostly run one standard single-agent scaffold. The clearest team comparison is Vals AI's October 2026 test on 50 apps: the lead received one generic instruction ("split the work into independent parts, delegate each one, then integrate and verify the result"), a cap of five concurrent sub-agents and no nesting, and chose everything else. The leads spawned 4.2 to 6.8 sub-agents per app; one of four score differences was significant (GPT 6 Sol at medium effort, 77.6% to 84.9%); and **teams cost 1.8 to 5.1 times as much**, up to $122 against $23.77 per app. Vals reported the chosen count as a descriptive statistic, did not equalise budget, and ran each setup once ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blog/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench)). METR gave each scaffold the same token budget when comparing vendor harnesses but has not tested multi-agent modes ([METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-02-13-measuring-time-horizon-using-claude-code-and-codex/)). Multi-agent safety benchmarks such as SCHEME fix the team and topology; nobody found tests whether a self-organised team colludes, although the UK AI Security Institute recorded independent agents under test leaving instructions for one another in an evaluation not designed for it ([SCHEME](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29178), [UK AISI](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/incident-report-unsanctioned-agent-behaviour-during-cyber-testing)).

Practice, in short, is split by purpose. Benchmarks that compare models prescribe. Products and third-party tests of products let the model choose under a cap and observe what it chose. Budgets are set per agent or by concurrency, with total tokens and dollars reported afterwards. No lab, evaluator or standards body scores the choice of structure against a budget, and the standards work under way (NIST's agent initiative, A2A, MCP) concerns interoperability and security, not team evaluation ([NIST](https://nist.gov/news-events/news/2026/02/announcing-ai-agent-standards-initiative-interoperable-and-secure)).

## SeedCo should headline prescribed ownership and add a model-chosen arm

The recommendations below are synthesis from the evidence above, fitted to SeedCo's design; no source states them in this form.

### Which conditions to run

**Keep the prescribed track as the headline and the training target.** SeedCo's premise is that the partition is imposed, as in a real company where ten owners exist whether or not that is the efficient way to make a change; ParallACT makes the same argument for treating the single agent as an upper reference and not a competitor ([ParallACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314)). Prescribed ownership is also what makes hidden boundary checks interpretable, since each check maps to a known pair of owners, and it gives training a stable assignment of credit. In this track, tell every agent its own services, the full roster of who owns what, the team size and the channel. The roster is a ready-made directory of who knows what, which the human literature links to team performance, and it leaves the measured behaviour where the benchmarks say agents are weakest: asking for and combining facts only another owner holds ([Bachrach et al.](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/transactive-memory-systems-in-context-a-meta-analytic-examination/), [Silo-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.01045v1)). Do not assign personas, job titles, a leader or a discussion protocol by default; each of those is a separate factor to test later, and a prescribed protocol is known to change results ([HiddenBench](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html)).

**Run team size as an evaluator-controlled sweep inside the prescribed track.** Grouping services into teams of three to five is a second level of the same factor, not a replacement for one owner per service. Keep the ten directories as the atomic units the sandbox enforces and build a grouped team by granting one agent several of them. The 3-to-5 range has support: human groups of three to five beat their best member on intellective problems where pairs do not, Claude Code's guidance is to start with three to five teammates, and agent benchmarks show success falling as interacting agents are added ([Laughlin et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16649860/), [Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams), [MineCollab](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.17950)). With one shared budget, ten agents also start with a tenth of it each. The cost of grouping is that a boundary inside one agent's holdings stops testing coordination, so every boundary check must be tagged per configuration as crossing agents or not. Organisation research predicts the informative failures sit on boundaries that cross owners ([Colfer and Baldwin](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/indcch/v25y2016i5p709-738..html)).

**Add a model-chosen track as a diagnostic on a task subset.** A lead agent receives the task, the map of ten owners and their directories, the total budget and one fixed generic instruction in the style Vals used, with no hint about count. It declares its organisation through tool calls that spawn an agent and grant it directories, so the sandbox still confines every agent and the harness logs the structure. Bound it the way products are bounded: depth one, and at most as many agents as there are owners. Doing everything itself is a legal choice. This places the existing conditions at the two ends of the lead's choice set, which yields a metric no benchmark reports: the shortfall of the chosen structure against the better of the solo and prescribed-team results on the same task and budget. That is DecisionBench's perfect-routing ceiling applied to team design ([DecisionBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19099)). A stricter variant in which the lead cannot write code, following SwarmBench and ClawArena-Team, isolates organising skill from the lead's own coding and is worth running once the first variant shows whether leads delegate at all ([SwarmBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.30661v1)).

The two tracks support different claims and should never be merged into one score. Prescribed results say how well a model coordinates inside a given organisation; model-chosen results say how well it designs one. The difference between them per model is a measurable structure benefit, and the capability-threshold result predicts its sign will flip from negative for weaker models to positive for stronger ones ([Dochkina](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28990v1)).

### What to hold fixed

Across every condition hold fixed the task and its hidden checks, the model and reasoning-effort setting, the per-agent scaffold and tools, the container-level confinement, the message channel, a wall-clock limit, and the total token budget counted inside the harness over every agent including the lead and all messages. Provider-side budget controls have artefacts that inflate apparent team gains, and per-agent limits, the lab convention, give teams more in total ([Tran and Kiela](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460), [Claude Opus 5 system card](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/b514064af1408018e64b1ad24e7d5e75850b4ffd/Claude+Opus+5+System+Card.pdf)). Fix one token-accounting rule and publish it, since cached input dominates team cost and ParallACT already weights cache reads and output differently ([ParallACT](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.00314)). Run at two or three budget levels, because the solo-versus-team ordering can change with budget and a single level hides that. Enforce ownership in the sandbox, never by prompt alone. Admit a task only if the solo agent can pass it and the no-messaging team cannot; the second test is what CooperBench lacked and the reason its messaging result was null ([CooperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13295)). Repeat each cell over several seeds: Vals ran once and found one significant difference in four, and only 16% of 445 reviewed benchmarks used statistical tests ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blog/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench), [Bean et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/1967e0fc3aa6cbbace562f5cb8e3954e-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.html)).

Two things should stay out of the headline. Per-agent blame from a model judge is unreliable: the best method identified the responsible agent 53.5% of the time on the Who&When dataset ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00212)). Judge-rated coordination quality is the least comparable metric in the field, and TeamBench's verifiers approved 49.4% of submissions that failed the grader ([TeamBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07073)). State on every result which of model, per-agent scaffold and team organisation was held fixed.

### Gaps SeedCo can fill

Five gaps in the existing work line up with SeedCo's design. **Service ownership with contracts**: no benchmark has agents own separate deployed services joined by contracts; NP-Bench's nine hand-built contract and fan-out scenarios are the nearest. **Partitioned read access**: no coding benchmark stops an agent reading another owner's source, which SeedCo's per-directory containers do. **Equal total budget**: no matched equal-token solo-versus-team result exists on a purpose-built team coding benchmark. **Team size on live repository tasks**: the purpose-built benchmarks fix two agents, and live runs stop at eight; a ten-owner company with grouped teams gives a size curve at constant budget. **Prescribed against model-chosen structure**: no controlled comparison exists on repository-scale software work, no benchmark scores the decision to delegate at all against a solo alternative, and no paper reports the team sizes frontier models choose against the best fixed size per task. Two smaller openings follow from the same data: a deterministic communication-cost metric, which exists outside coding but not in it, and a record of whether self-organised teams accept each other's unverified claims, which the Opus 5 card names as uncovered.

## Conclusion

The question "tell the model its team, or let it decide" turns out to be a question about what is being measured, and the literature has been answering it by accident: benchmark authors prescribe because they want to compare models, and product builders let the model choose because they want the task done. Neither group has measured the difference. SeedCo's sandbox is unusually well placed to do so, because ownership there is a property of the containers and not of the prompt, so a prescribed team, a grouped team and a model-chosen team are the same mechanism with different grants, and the solo agent is the limiting case of all three.

The risk to plan for is that the model-chosen arm is uninformative at first. If ten services fit in one context, a capable lead that keeps everything for itself will tie the solo condition, and the evidence on under-delegation says many will. That outcome is itself a result worth reporting, and it is the argument for keeping prescribed ownership as the headline: the imposed partition is what creates the coordination problem, and the model-chosen arm shows whether a model, given the freedom, would have created that problem for itself. The open empirical step is a pilot on a handful of tasks that checks three orderings at equal budget: solo above the messaging team, the messaging team above the no-messaging team, and the model-chosen result against both ends.
